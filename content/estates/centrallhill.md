![Central Hill estate image](src/images/centrallhill.jpg)

The Central Hill Estate in Gypsy Hill was built over two phases from 1967-1974. Unusually for its time, it was designed by female architect Rosemary Stjernstedt, under the guidance of Lambeth’s chief Town Planner, Ted Hollamby, and is an iconic example of London’s later Modernist social housing. Upon completion, the estate consisted of 476 one-, three- and four-bedroom housing units.  

Central Hill is the largest of Lambeth's [six estates](/underthreat/lambeth) earmarked for demolition, having been designated as brownfield land by Lambeth Council in 2017. 

It was first earmarked for potential redevelopment in a [December 2014 Cabinet decision](https://moderngov.lambeth.gov.uk/documents/s70441/03_Lambeth%20Estate%20Regeneration%20and%20Housing%20Delivery%20-%20December%202014%20v3%20docx.pdf). Plans had envisaged the demolition of the entire estate and their replacement with 725 new flats, the overwhelming majority of which will be sold at market rate, but after years of community campaigning, the council is potentially reconsidering demolition. 

In November 2020, Lambeth approved a 'kick-start' initial phase of redevelopment involving the demolition of Truslove House, a block which had been initially designed with 6 studio apartments to house trainee nurses and a two-bed flat for a matron. As of January 2025, Lambeth Council has demolished this 4-storey block and the new build decant block has been constructed in its place. This block consists of 31 flats, all council rent, although the initial intention was for 9 of these to be shared ownership. There are therefore now only 464 homes on the estate being threatened with demolition. 

Despite Truslove house being owned by the council, in April 2020 the real estate services company Savills applied for planning permission  to demolish the structure and replace it with a part 5- and part 7-storey structure housing 31 affordable housing units. This was approved by Lambeth’s Planning Applications Committee in January 2021, has been completed and is now called “Rosemary Heights”. This development was initially intended to consist of a mixture of shared ownership and socially rented (9 and 22 respectively) properties, but now is 100% socially rented, as Lambeth Council is keen to emphasise. Only three parking spaces have been provided and other residents have been permitted to park elsewhere on the estate. In total, the demolition and rebuild on this site amounted to £14 million, or £452,000 per housing unit. 

Lambeth [had said](https://moderngov.lambeth.gov.uk/documents/s87425/Cabinet%20Report%20-%20Central%20Hill%20-%20March%202017%20-%20final%20clean%204%20RD.pdf) that it is demolishing the estate because the cost of refurbishing the existing homes is £44,000 per dwelling when its average cost for other estates is only £16,000 and that the Council simply doesn't have the £18.5m required, but the price of construction Rosemary Heights calls this into question. 

![Central Hill estate image](src/images/centrallhillrefurb.png)

In 2019, an [FOI request revealed](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/581698/response/1393236/attach/3/Attachment.pdf) that Lambeth is estimated to be spending £94m alone on buying back leasehold properties across its 6 estate demolitions. The Save Central Hill Campaign believes that at least 90 units have been purchased by the council on the estate, a large number of which have been deliberately kept empty. For example, one 3-bedroom property was bought back by the council for £440,000 just to be boarded up.

Whilst the decant and re-housing programme started in 2016, the estate remains fully occupied with [void units being let out at full market rents or as temporary accommodation at LHA rates](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/581698/response/1393236/attach/4/Attachment%202.pdf). At one point 63 housing units were being rented out at market rates. As of January 2025, a number of these tenants have now moved off the estate due to the impossibility of a renewal of contract. The council has also begun issuing Section 21 (No-fault Eviction) notices to these tenants. The council claims to be using this to house the homeless but this makes no sense given the number of empty properties.

The estate is not one of those listed as having agreed [grant funding](/approved/funding) from the mayor or that has been [exempted](https://www.london.gov.uk/programmes-strategies/housing-and-land/homes-londoners/estate-regeneration/estate-regeneration-data) from his requirement to ballot residents on the demolition of their homes.

In early 2023, Lambeth Council announced that is was pausing the redevelopment of Central Hill and two other estates (Fenwick and Cressingham Gardens) after a [critical report](https://moderngov.lambeth.gov.uk/documents/s142089/Kerslake%20Review%20into%20Affordable%20Housing%20in%20Lambeth.pdf) recommended a ‘fundamental reset’ to the way it handles the long-running projects.

In September 2024, Lambeth published a report highlighting the Council’s plans to reset key estate renewal projects and regain control of properties previously under HfL’s management.

The Kerslake Review, which took place in December 2022, was a pivotal moment for the Council’s housing strategy.

Two primary recommendations emerged: first, to dissolve Homes for Lambeth and return the Council’s social housing delivery to in-house management, and second, to address long-standing failures in engaging with residents.

These recommendations have led to a fundamental reshaping of Lambeth’s estate regeneration plans, effectively putting many estates back to square one.

The Overview and Scrutiny report indicates that estates such as Central Hill, Cressingham Gardens, and Fenwick are facing a complete reset.

In December 2024, it was [reported](https://londonnewsonline.co.uk/news/lambeth-to-take-stock-of-gipsy-hill-estate-before-deciding-whether-to-stick-or-twist/) that the Council is to undertake a stock condition survey of the estate to inform a further options appraisal.
